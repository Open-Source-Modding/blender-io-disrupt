**Havok** is a [middleware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middleware) [software suite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_suite) developed by the Irish company [Havok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havok_(company)) (originally **Telekynesis Research Limited**). Havok provides [physics engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_engine), navigation, and cloth simulation components that can be integrated into [video game engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_engine).

In 2007, [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_Corporation) acquired Havok Inc.[[2]](#cite_note-2) In 2008, Havok was honored at the 59th Annual [Technology & Engineering Emmy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology_&_Engineering_Emmy_Award) for advancing the development of physics engines in electronic entertainment. In 2015, [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft) acquired Havok.[[3]](#cite_note-3)

## History

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Havok_(software)&action=edit&section=1)]

Telekynesis Research Limited was founded in 1998 by two university students Hugh Reynolds and [Steven Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Collins) of the [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) department in [Trinity College, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Dublin) who would form the studio based in Dublin, Ireland. The software was originally named **Omniate** initially, but was then renamed to Havok (Named after the [Marvel X-Men character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havok_(character))).

Version 1.0 of the Havok [SDK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_development_kit) was unveiled at the [Game Developers Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Developers_Conference) (GDC) in March 2000.[[4]](#cite_note-4)

### Ipion Virtual Physics

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Havok_(software)&action=edit&section=2)]

In 1999, a German-software company based in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich) had developed their own physics engine called **Ipion Virtual Physics**, it was a real-time middleware [physics engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_engine) developed in the late 1990s by the German software company Ipion Software. Designed for the simulation of rigid body dynamics in video games and interactive media, it was a significant early competitor in the physics middleware market along with [MathEngine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MathEngine?action=edit&redlink=1).[[5]](#cite_note-5) In June 2000, Ipion was acquired by Havok, and its core technologies were subsequently integrated into the **[Havok Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havok_Physics)** engine.[[6]](#cite_note-6)[[7]](#cite_note-7)

Ipion was founded in 1998. The development of the Virtual Physics engine was led by chief architect Oliver Strunk and a decentralized team of engineers across Germany. Developed in C++, the engine was built with cross-platform compatibility in mind, supporting Microsoft Windows, Linux, Solaris, and the PlayStation 2 console.

On June 8, 2000, the Irish company [Havok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havok_(software)) acquired Ipion. This merger transformed the Ipion team into Havok's Munich office, marking the company's third international location after Dublin and Palo Alto. The acquisition was a pivotal moment in the industry, as the sophisticated algorithms of the Ipion Virtual Physics SDK became the technological foundation for Havok's 1.5 release and beyond.[[8]](#cite_note-8)[[9]](#cite_note-auto-9)

Ipion was noted for its advanced handling of complex physical interactions for the era. Its architecture included:

- **Collision Detection:** A multi-phase system that optimized performance by utilizing bounding volumes before proceeding to detailed geometric checks.

- **Constraint Systems:** The SDK allowed developers to define kinematic pairs with specific degrees of freedom. This included native support for complex joints such as ball-and-socket and hinge joints.

- **Dynamic Controllers:** Beyond simple impulses, the engine allowed for persistent forces through various "controllers," enabling simulations of magnets, springs, and customized user-defined forces.

- **Pipeline Integration:** While the SDK did not include proprietary 3D exporters, it was uniquely compatible with the *Quake II* .BSP file format, allowing developers to integrate physics directly into established level design pipelines.[[9]](#cite_note-auto-9)[[10]](#cite_note-10)

At the time of its commercial peak, the Ipion SDK was licensed for approximately $50,000 to $60,000 per project. The software package provided developers with a 76-page technical manual and a comprehensive C++ library.

Ipion's most visible legacy is found in the 3DMark 2001 SE benchmark. The software's "Game 4" and "Physics Feature" tests utilized the Ipion engine to demonstrate high-end hardware capabilities through complex rigid body simulations. It was also utilized in several retail titles, including the 2001 racing game *Route 66* (known internationally as *USA Racer*). Following the Havok merger, the Ipion name was phased out, though its influence persists in modern physics simulations through its direct contribution to the Havok engine.

It is also famously used in *[Half-Life 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-Life_2)*, albeit heavily modified by Valve as VPhysics (VPhysics uses custom proprietary code from Valve, with additional [Havok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havok_(software)) code for constraints and memory optimized partial polytype).[[11]](#cite_note-11) *Half-Life 2* is lauded as one of the early licensees of a real-time physics middleware such as the Ipion Virtual Physics early in its development cycle at the start of the millennium.

## Products

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Havok_(software)&action=edit&section=4)]
The Havok middleware suite consists of the following modules:

- **Havok Physics**:[[13]](#cite_note-13) Originally from Ipion Software ([Ipion Virtual Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipion_Virtual_Physics?action=edit&redlink=1)), it is designed primarily for [video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game), and allows for [real-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_computing) collision and [dynamics of rigid bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body_dynamics) in three dimensions. It provides multiple types of [dynamic constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multibody_dynamics) between rigid bodies (e.g. for [ragdoll physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragdoll_physics)), and has a highly optimized [collision detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision_detection) library. By using [dynamical simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_simulation), Havok Physics allows for more realistic virtual worlds in games. The company was developing a specialized version of Havok Physics called Havok FX that made use of [ATI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATI_Technologies) and [Nvidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia) GPUs for physics simulations,[[14]](#cite_note-14) but the goal of GPU acceleration did not materialize until several years later.[[15]](#cite_note-15)

- **Havok Navigation**:[[16]](#cite_note-16) In 2009, Havok released Havok AI, which provides advanced [pathfinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathfinding) capabilities for games. Havok AI provides navigation mesh generation, pathfinding and path following for video game environments. In 2024, this product was renamed to Havok Navigation.[[17]](#cite_note-17)

- **Havok Cloth**:[[18]](#cite_note-18) Released in 2008, Havok Cloth deals with efficient [simulation of character garments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloth_modeling) and [soft body dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_body_dynamics).

- **Havok Destruction (discontinued)**: Also released in 2008, Havok Destruction provides tools for creation of [destructible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destructible_environment) and deformable rigid body environments.

- **Havok Animation Studio (discontinued)**: Havok Animation Studio is formally known as Havok Behavior and Havok Animation. Havok Behavior is a runtime SDK for controlling game character animation at a high level using [finite-state machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_machine). Havok Animation provides efficient playback and compression of character animations in games, and features such as [inverse kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_kinematics).

- **Havok Script (discontinued)**: Havok Script is a [Lua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lua_(programming_language))-compatible [virtual machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_machine) designed for [video game development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_development). It is shipped as part of the Havok Script Studio.
